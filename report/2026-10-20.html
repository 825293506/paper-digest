Nonlocal metasurfaces enable all-optical processing of spatial information in optical fields. Here, we demonstrate a topology-optimised metasurface that switches between phase-contrast and brightfield imaging modalities via polarisation control, eliminating the need to reposition optical components or use computational techniques to image transparent samples. Specifically, for one polarisation state, an asymmetric transfer function about normal incidence performs a first order derivative on the phase, producing pseudo-3D phase-contrast images of transparent biological samples while the orthogonal state returns the result of the identity operator. This work extends inverse-design methods to reconfigurable phase-contrast microscopy and quantitative analogue optical computation with applications in biological imaging, medical diagnostics, and materials characterisation.

Abstract Metasurfaces are considered the backbone of the ongoing photonics revolution to shape the electromagnetic waves on demand via a flat array of resonators. The beauty of metasurfaces lies in their ability to simultaneously control multiple waves. However, obtaining such multi-functionality often requires active components, such as active electronics and complex tuning mechanisms. A recent study demonstrates a fully passive architecture, composed of sliding metasurfaces that combines two distinct functionalities, i.e. frequency-selective filtering with wide-angle beam steering in a single platform. By replacing local phase reconfiguration with a simple geometric translation between complementary phase profiles, the work introduces a simple yet innovative route to a two-dimensional frequency-angle selection window. It opens new opportunities for passive wave-based information processing.
