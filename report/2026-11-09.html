Every reconfigurable microwave device places a tuning element - a varactor, a diode, transistor, microelectromechanical or phase-change switch, a ferroelectric or liquid-crystal load - inside a fixed passive network. The network can make a small change of the element produce a large change of the response, and design practice often credits it with the device's tuning capability. This paper shows that a fixed, linear, passive network cannot add to that capability. Each tuning element carries a tuning budget, a dimensionless length computed from its own impedance trajectory, and a fixed passive network can transmit that budget or lose part of it, never enlarge it. The unit of the budget is the hyperbolic distance of the Smith chart: the distance from a match is the logarithm of the voltage standing-wave ratio, and the distance between two states sets their mutual mismatch and is the logarithm of their commutation quality factor. Abstract Dielectric nanoparticles can be engineered to scatter light predominantly in the transverse direction, perpendicular to the incident plane wave’s propagation direction, a phenomenon known as the transverse Kerker effect. Although complete cancellation of forward scattering from a single object is forbidden by the optical theorem, we show that a single photonic mode can provide an ideal transverse Kerker effect. When combining several nanoparticles to form a metasurface, the mode evolves into an accidental bound state in the continuum. This enables polarization-independent quasi bound states in the continuum whose quality factors are tunable without symmetry breaking. We experimentally demonstrate our concept in the visible, achieving the first polarization-independent bound state in the continuum without Brillouin-zone folding. These modes maintain large quality factors over a broader region of momentum space than conventional bound states in the continuum. Our results establish a platform for realizing ultranarrow resonances free of conventional design constraints.
